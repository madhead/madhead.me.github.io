    How Herdr broke my sound

    Posted on 2026-11-12 3 mins to read

    Imagine: you install a new terminal tool, reboot your laptop, and suddenly your audio is gone. PulseAudio refuses to start, the volume mixer hangs on "Establishing connection to PulseAudio. Please wait…", and your system settings show zero audio outputs.

    I don’t even need to do that, as it happened to me. How does a terminal multiplexer manage to nuke PulseAudio?

    Watch closely.

    The good intentions of herdr-core

    I recently started playing with Herdr, a modern terminal multiplexer. Like many background daemons, herdr-core wants to be helpful and survive your logout so your sessions don’t get abruptly terminated.

    To achieve this under systemd, it attempts to enable lingering for your user account. In fact, if you poke around inside the binary, you’ll find it running:

    loginctl --no-ask-password enable-linger <username>

    Under Ubuntu, default PolicyKit rules happily allow an active desktop session to enable lingering for oneself without prompting for a password (self-linger). So herdr-core quietly turned lingering on behind my back.

    What’s lingering, anyway?

    Normally, systemd user services (systemctl --user) only start when you actually log in.

    When linger is enabled, systemd starts your user manager right when the machine boots up, before anyone even touches the keyboard or reaches the display manager.

    Sounds harmless enough until you combine it with an encrypted home directory.

    The catch: encrypted home directories

    My home directory is encrypted. It only gets decrypted and mounted when PAM handles my login.

    Here is the exact boot-time disaster:

    1. Machine boots up. systemd sees Linger=yes for me and immediately attempts to spawn all my stuff.

    2. At this point, my home is still locked and unmounted.

    3. System-level user units, living in /usr/lib/systemd/user/, try to start. Among them there is pulseaudio.service.

    4. PulseAudio tries to access ~/.config/pulse. Since ~ is locked, it fails…

    5. systemd retries it a few more times during only 3 (!!) seconds, hits the start rate limit, throws its hands in the air, and completely disables PulseAudio.

    6. I finally log in, my home is decrypted. But systemd has already given up on PulseAudio!

    Collateral damage

    To make matters worse, PulseAudio wasn’t the only victim.

    systemd couldn’t see ~/.config/systemd/user either. When I finally logged in, all my custom user stuff was completely broken: systemd doesn’t automatically run a daemon-reload or re-evaluate target dependencies.

    The Fix

    I don’t need Herdr sessions to survive logouts on my local machine. But simply running loginctl disable-linger wouldn’t stick the next time herdr-core starts (remember: it sets self-linger on every start!).

    The cleanest permanent fix is to tell PolicyKit to outright reject automatic linger toggling.

    So, I dropped a rule into /etc/polkit-1/localauthority/50-local.d/disallow-linger.pkla:

    [Disallow enable-linger]
Identity=unix-user:*
Action=org.freedesktop.login1.set-self-linger;org.freedesktop.login1.set-user-linger
ResultAny=no
ResultInactive=no
ResultActive=no

    Now:

    • When herdr-core attempts loginctl --no-ask-password enable-linger, PolicyKit immediately blocks it.

    • Linger=no stays.

    • On reboot — just as previously — user services wait for PAM to mount /home before starting.

    • PulseAudio and user timers work now.

    Oh, the endless wonders that AI brings into our lives. You just wanted an agent-friendly terminal, and next thing you do is reverse-engineering PolicyKit and reading assembly offsets at 3 AM to get your headphones to play sound again. With AI, of course!

    What a time to be alive.
